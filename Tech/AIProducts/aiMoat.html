Where the Moat Really Is in AI Products

Why the model is not your defensibility, and what is

Posted by Syed Zain Raza on September 6th, 2026

Founders and product teams keep asking the wrong question about AI defensibility: "which model should we build on?" The uncomfortable truth is that the model is the least defensible part of the whole stack. It is a rented commodity available to you and every competitor at the same API. If your entire product is a thin wrapper around a prompt, a better-funded team - or the model provider itself - can replicate it in a weekend. The moat has to come from somewhere else.

Why the Model Is Not the Moat

The frontier models are extraordinary and getting better fast, but that is precisely the problem for defensibility: everyone gets the upgrade at once. When a new model ships, your clever prompt engineering around the old one's weaknesses becomes obsolete, and so does the equivalent work your competitor did. Building your moat on the model is building on a foundation that the vendor rebuilds under you every few months. You benefit from the rising tide, but so does everyone, which means no one is ahead.

Moat 1: Proprietary Data and the Feedback Loop

The most durable AI moat is data your competitors cannot get, feeding a loop that compounds. Not the public data every model already trained on, but the private data your product generates: which outputs users kept, edited, or rejected; the corrections they made; the outcomes that followed. A product that captures this turns usage into improvement - better evals, better retrieval, better fine-tuning targets - so the product gets smarter the more it is used, and a new entrant starts from zero on a loop you have been compounding for years. This is the flywheel that actually defends.

Moat 2: Workflow Integration and Switching Cost

A model is easy to swap. A tool that is woven into how a team works every day is not. When your product holds the user's history, connects to their other systems, encodes their process, and has become the place the work happens, the cost of leaving is measured in disruption, not just dollars. The moat is not the intelligence; it is the integration. The deeper the product sits in the workflow, the less the underlying model matters to retention.

Moat 3: Distribution

In a world where the core capability is a commodity, getting to the customer is disproportionately valuable. An incumbent with an existing user base can bolt AI onto a product people already use and reach a million users overnight, while a superior standalone tool struggles to be found. Distribution has always mattered; in the AI era, where the technology gap between competitors is small and shrinking, it may matter more than the technology itself.

Moat 4: Trust and Domain Depth

For serious, high-stakes uses, being the tool a profession trusts is its own moat. That trust is earned slowly - through reliability, through domain-specific accuracy, through handling the edge cases that a general tool fumbles - and it does not transfer to a newcomer just because they have the same base model. Depth in a specific domain, and the reputation that comes with it, is defensible precisely because it cannot be prompted into existence.

Commodity (not a moat):   the base model, generic prompting
Durable moats:            proprietary data + feedback loop
                          workflow integration + switching cost
                          distribution / existing reach
                          domain trust and accuracy

The Takeaway

Treat the model as the electricity, not the product. It powers everything and differentiates nothing. Your defensibility lives in the things the model cannot supply: the private data loop that makes you smarter with every use, the workflow you become indispensable to, the distribution that gets you to users, and the trust you earn in a domain. Build the product so that even if a competitor had the identical model tomorrow - and they will - they would still be years behind on the things that actually keep customers.
