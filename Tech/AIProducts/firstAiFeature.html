Shipping Your First AI Feature

A practical 0-to-1 playbook for adding AI to an existing product

Posted by Syed Zain Raza on September 8th, 2026

You have an existing product and pressure to "add AI." The failure mode is obvious and common: a team picks the flashiest possible feature, builds a demo, ships it, and watches it break in ways they did not anticipate. Here is a more reliable path - a practical sequence for getting a first AI feature from idea to something users actually trust, without betting the company on it.

Step 1: Pick the Right First Use Case

The best first AI feature is boring, high-volume, low-stakes, and tolerant of imperfection. You want a task where the model does language-shaped work that is currently manual toil, where a wrong answer is cheap and easily corrected, and where you will get lots of usage to learn from. Summarizing, drafting, extracting, classifying, and answering questions over your own content are all strong candidates. Avoid, as a first feature, anything where a mistake is expensive or irreversible - that is where you graduate to, not where you start.

Good first feature:  high volume, low stakes, language work,
                     easy to correct, easy to measure
Bad first feature:   rare, high stakes, must-be-exact,
                     hard to undo, hard to evaluate

Step 2: Prototype Fast, but Collect Real Inputs

Build the quick version to prove the idea is feasible - a prompt against a capable model is fine. But the moment it works on your examples, start gathering real inputs: the messy, varied, unglamorous things actual users will feed it. Those inputs are worth more than the prototype, because they are the difference between a demo and a product, and they become your eval set.

Step 3: Build the Eval Harness Before You Scale

Before you invest in polish, invest in measurement. Assemble twenty to fifty representative cases, including the hard ones and the failures you have already seen, and a way to score outputs - deterministic checks where you can, an LLM-as-judge with a clear rubric where you cannot, and human review to calibrate. This harness is what lets every future change be an informed decision instead of a guess. It is the highest-leverage thing you will build, and teams consistently skip it until it is too late.

Step 4: Ship Behind a Flag, With a Human in the Loop

Do not launch to everyone. Ship to a small slice behind a feature flag, and for anything consequential, keep the output as a suggestion a human approves rather than an action the system takes. Instrument everything: log inputs and outputs, capture a one-tap "not helpful" signal, and watch cost and latency per use. The goal of this stage is not scale; it is learning safely, with a kill switch.

Step 5: Close the Loop

Now the flywheel starts. The failures you logged become new eval cases. The corrections users made become training signal. You tune the prompt, tighten retrieval, adjust the model choice, and re-run the evals to confirm each change actually helped. Cost comes down as you cache and route; quality goes up as the eval set grows. Only once the numbers are good and stable do you widen the rollout.

The Meta-Lesson

The teams that ship durable AI features are not the ones with the grandest vision; they are the ones who chose a humble first target, measured obsessively, shipped carefully behind a flag, and turned every real failure into an improvement. Start small, keep a human in the loop, build the eval harness first, and let the product earn its way to more responsibility. That discipline is what separates an AI feature that is still used in a year from one that was quietly turned off after the launch buzz faded.
